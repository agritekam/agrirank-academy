1. Introduction & Importance

Tomato is one of the most important vegetable crops of the world. It is cultivated for fresh consumption as well as processing into juice, puree, paste, ketchup, sauce, canned products and dehydrated products.

Tomato is nutritionally important because the fruit contains vitamins, minerals, organic acids, sugars, antioxidants and carotenoid pigments, particularly lycopene.

Scientific name: Solanum lycopersicum L.
Family: Solanaceae
Chromosome number: 2n = 24
Fruit type: Berry
Pollination: Predominantly self-pollinated

2. Origin, Centre of Origin & History

  • Tomato is native to the western region of South America.
  • The Andean region of Peru, Ecuador and neighbouring areas is associated with its origin.
  • Mexico played an important role in domestication and subsequent spread of tomato.
  • Tomato was later introduced into Europe and subsequently spread to other parts of the world.
  • Modern cultivated tomato is associated with Solanum lycopersicum.

Important Exam Point

For examination purposes, remember the association of tomato with Andean South America and Mexico in its domestication history.

4. Botanical Description

Root

  • Tomato develops a strong root system when directly seeded.
  • Transplanted plants develop a more branched fibrous root system.
  • Adventitious roots may develop from the stem when portions of the stem remain in moist soil.

Stem

  • Stem is herbaceous when young and becomes somewhat woody with age.
  • It is branched and may require support in indeterminate cultivars.
  • Stem produces lateral branches from axillary buds.

Leaves

  • Leaves are alternate and compound.
  • They possess characteristic tomato odour due to glandular structures.

Inflorescence

  • Flowers are generally borne in clusters.
  • The inflorescence is commonly described as a cyme.

Flower

  • Flowers are generally yellow.
  • Flowers are bisexual.
  • Tomato is predominantly self-pollinated.

Fruit

  • Botanically the tomato fruit is a berry.
  • Fruit shape varies from round to flattened, oval, elongated and other forms.
  • Fruit colour changes during ripening because of changes in pigments.

Seed

  • Seeds are small and flattened.
  • They are covered with a mucilaginous coating.

5. Types of Tomato

Determinate type

  • Plants have relatively limited vegetative growth.
  • Flowering and fruiting occur over a comparatively concentrated period.
  • Suitable for many processing and concentrated harvest systems.

Indeterminate type

  • Vegetative growth continues for a longer period under favourable conditions.
  • Plants generally require staking, training or trellising.
  • Harvesting continues over a longer period.

Semi-determinate type

These plants show characteristics intermediate between determinate and indeterminate growth habits.

Area

Area means the land under tomato cultivation, generally expressed in hectares.

Production

Production means the total quantity of tomato harvested, generally expressed in tonnes.

Productivity

Productivity means production obtained per unit area. A commonly used expression is tonnes per hectare.

Formula:
Productivity = Total Production ÷ Total Area

Important Production Belts

Major tomato-growing belts occur in southern, central, eastern and western parts of India. Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, Maharashtra, Gujarat, West Bengal and other states contribute substantially to national production.

9. Climate Requirement

  • Tomato is a warm-season crop but performs best under moderate temperatures.
  • Good sunlight is essential for healthy growth and fruit quality.
  • Very high temperature can reduce pollen viability and fruit set.
  • Very low temperature can also interfere with flowering and fruit development.
  • High humidity combined with prolonged leaf wetness favours several diseases.

Critical Temperature Effects

  • Extreme heat may cause flower drop.
  • High night temperature may reduce fruit set.
  • Cold conditions may delay growth and flowering.
  • Temperature fluctuations can influence fruit cracking and quality.

10. Soil Requirement

  • Tomato grows well in fertile, well-drained soils.
  • Loam and sandy-loam soils are generally suitable.
  • Good organic matter improves soil structure and water-holding capacity.
  • Waterlogging should be avoided.
  • Soil should be free from serious disease and nematode problems whenever possible.

11. Seed Selection & Nursery Management

Seed Selection

  • Use genetically pure and healthy seed.
  • Seed should have good germination and vigour.
  • Prefer varieties/hybrids suited to the target season and region.
  • Disease-resistant cultivars should be preferred wherever available.

Nursery Preparation

  • Nursery site should have good drainage and adequate sunlight.
  • Use clean nursery media.
  • Avoid overcrowding of seedlings.
  • Provide balanced moisture.
  • Protect seedlings from damping-off and insect vectors.
  • Seedling hardening should be done before transplanting.

Seedling Hardening

Gradual reduction of excess water and adjustment of seedlings to field conditions helps seedlings tolerate transplanting shock.

12. Transplanting, Spacing & Plant Population

  • Transplant healthy, vigorous and hardened seedlings.
  • Spacing varies with variety, season, soil fertility and training system.
  • Closer spacing can increase plant population but may reduce aeration if excessive.
  • Wider spacing may improve aeration but can reduce plants per unit area.
  • Proper spacing is important for light interception, disease management and fruit quality.
Exam Point: Do not memorize one spacing value for every tomato crop. Recommended spacing changes with variety/hybrid and production system.

13. Irrigation Management

  • Tomato requires adequate and relatively uniform soil moisture.
  • Moisture stress during flowering and fruit development can reduce yield.
  • Excess water can cause poor aeration, nutrient problems and disease development.
  • Drip irrigation improves water-use efficiency and allows fertigation.
  • Mulching helps reduce evaporation and suppress weeds.

Critical Stages

  • Seedling establishment
  • Flowering
  • Fruit setting
  • Fruit enlargement

14. Nutrient Management

Tomato is a nutrient-responsive crop. Balanced nutrition is essential for vegetative growth, flowering, fruit setting, fruit development and quality.

Major Nutrients

  • Nitrogen: promotes vegetative growth but excessive nitrogen may delay maturity and favour excessive foliage.
  • Phosphorus: important for root development and reproductive growth.
  • Potassium: important for fruit development, quality and plant physiological processes.

Secondary and Micronutrients

  • Calcium is important for cell-wall development and fruit quality.
  • Magnesium is a component of chlorophyll.
  • Boron and other micronutrients are required in small quantities.
  • Deficiencies should be diagnosed before corrective application.
Best Practice: Use soil testing, crop requirement, recommended regional fertilizer schedule and split application/fertigation where appropriate rather than applying nutrients blindly.

15. Intercultural Operations

  • Weed control during early crop growth is important.
  • Mulching reduces weed emergence and conserves moisture.
  • Earthing-up may be useful in appropriate production systems.
  • Removal of severely diseased plant parts helps sanitation.
  • Staking and training improve canopy management in indeterminate plants.

16. Staking, Training & Pruning

Staking

Plants are supported with stakes, strings or trellises to keep foliage and fruit away from the ground and improve light penetration and air movement.

Training

Training directs plant growth along the support system. It is especially important in indeterminate tomato under protected or intensive systems.

Pruning

Selective removal of unwanted shoots or leaves may be practiced depending on the production system. Excessive pruning should be avoided because healthy leaves are required for photosynthesis.

17. Flowering, Pollination & Fruit Set

  • Tomato flowers are bisexual.
  • Self-pollination is predominant.
  • Temperature, humidity and pollen viability influence fruit set.
  • Extreme heat can cause poor pollen viability and flower drop.
  • Good plant nutrition and water management support fruit setting.
  • In protected cultivation, mechanical or assisted pollination may be used where required.

18. Physiological Disorders

Blossom End Rot

A dark, sunken lesion develops near the blossom end of the fruit. It is associated with inadequate calcium supply to rapidly developing fruit and is often aggravated by irregular water availability.

Management: Maintain uniform soil moisture, avoid severe water stress, maintain balanced nutrition and correct calcium availability based on diagnosis.

Fruit Cracking

Cracks develop on the fruit surface, often following rapid fruit growth after fluctuations in moisture availability.

Management: Maintain uniform irrigation, avoid severe moisture fluctuation, use suitable varieties and maintain balanced nutrition.

Puffiness

Fruits may have poorly developed locules and appear light or hollow. It may be associated with poor fertilization and environmental or nutritional conditions.

Sunscald

Exposed portions of fruit may develop pale, yellow or damaged areas due to excessive solar radiation and high temperature.

Management: Maintain adequate foliage cover and avoid unnecessary removal of healthy leaves.

Blotchy Ripening

Uneven colour development occurs on fruit. Environmental conditions, nutrition and cultivar characteristics can influence the disorder.

19. Weed Management

  • Maintain a weed-free crop during early growth.
  • Hand weeding and intercultivation can be used where suitable.
  • Mulching suppresses weed emergence.
  • Herbicides may be used only according to crop-specific label recommendations.
  • Integrated weed management is preferred over dependence on a single method.

20. Major Insect Pests & Complete Management

A. Tomato Fruit Borer – Helicoverpa armigera

Identification & Damage

  • Larvae feed on leaves, flowers and especially developing fruits.
  • Larvae bore into fruits and leave characteristic holes.
  • Frass may be visible around the entry hole.
  • Damaged fruits become unsuitable for fresh market.

Cultural Management

  • Remove and destroy infested fruits.
  • Maintain field sanitation.
  • Remove weeds that can harbour the pest.
  • Follow crop rotation where practical.

Mechanical / Physical Management

  • Hand-pick and destroy visible larvae and damaged fruits.
  • Use appropriate monitoring traps.
  • Use light traps where suitable as part of an integrated programme.

Biological Management

  • Conserve natural enemies.
  • Use suitable parasitoids and predators where recommended.
  • Microbial biopesticides such as Bt or HaNPV may be incorporated into IPM according to local recommendations.

Botanical Management

  • Neem-based products can be used as part of an IPM programme where recommended.

Chemical Management

  • Use only registered, label-recommended insecticides for tomato and the target pest.
  • Rotate insecticides with different modes of action to reduce resistance development.
  • Observe label dose, waiting period and safety precautions.
IPM: Regular monitoring + removal of infested fruits + natural enemy conservation + microbial/biopesticide options + need-based selective insecticide use.
