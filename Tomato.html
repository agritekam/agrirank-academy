1. Introduction

Tomato is one of the most important vegetable crops of the world. It is widely cultivated for fresh consumption as well as for processing into products such as puree, paste, ketchup, sauce and juice.

Tomato belongs to the family Solanaceae and is an important warm-season vegetable crop. The crop has high economic importance because of its nutritional value, wide adaptability and large processing industry.

🌍 Origin & Distribution

Tomato originated in the western region of South America and was subsequently spread to different parts of the world. Today it is cultivated widely in tropical, subtropical and temperate regions.

2. Plant Description

Tomato is a herbaceous plant with a highly branched shoot system, well-developed root system, compound leaves, yellow flowers and fleshy berry fruits. Its growth habit and branching pattern vary with genotype and cultivation system.

Complete tomato plant showing major vegetative and reproductive parts.

🔍 Identify the Plant: Root → Stem → Leaf → Flower → Fruit

🌱 2.1 Root System

Tomato develops a well-branched root system. When plants are raised from seed and transplanted, the original primary root may be disturbed and a large number of lateral and adventitious roots can develop. The root system is important for anchorage, absorption of water and mineral nutrients, and storage of assimilates.

Important Features

  • Root system is extensively branched.
  • Primary and lateral roots form a network in the soil.
  • Adventitious roots can develop from the stem, especially when stem portions are in contact with moist soil.
  • Good root development is important for vigorous vegetative growth and fruit production.
  • Root development is strongly influenced by soil moisture, aeration, temperature and soil structure.

Major Root Regions

  • Primary root: The main root developing from the radicle.
  • Lateral roots: Branches arising from the main and other roots.
  • Root hairs: Fine structures that increase the effective absorbing surface.
  • Root tip: Actively growing region involved in root extension.

Tomato root architecture and root development reference.

🎯 PG/JRF Point: Tomato can develop adventitious roots from stem portions under suitable moist conditions. This is one reason why proper transplanting and appropriate planting depth can influence root establishment.

🌿 2.2 Stem

The tomato stem is herbaceous and becomes more complex as the plant develops. It bears leaves, lateral branches, inflorescences and fruits. The stem contains vascular tissues responsible for transport of water, minerals and photosynthates.

Stem Characteristics

  • Young stems are green and herbaceous.
  • The stem bears nodes and internodes.
  • Leaves arise at nodes.
  • Axillary buds occur in the leaf axils.
  • Branches develop from lateral/axillary buds.
  • Trichomes are present on the stem surface.
  • Older portions may undergo secondary growth.

Growth Habit

Tomato shows a characteristic branching pattern in which the shoot changes from initial monopodial growth to a sympodial pattern after flowering begins. Cultivars also differ in their overall growth habit.

  • Determinate: Vegetative growth terminates after a limited number of flowering/branching units and the plant remains relatively compact.
  • Indeterminate: Vegetative growth continues for a longer period and the plant continues producing leaves and flowering branches.
  • Semi-determinate: Intermediate growth behaviour.

Node, Internode & Axillary Bud

  • Node: Point on the stem where leaves and axillary buds arise.
  • Internode: Stem portion between two successive nodes.
  • Axillary bud: Bud present in the axil of a leaf that can develop into a lateral shoot.

🎯 Remember: Tomato's later shoot development shows a sympodial branching pattern. Determinate and indeterminate growth habits are important concepts in tomato production and training.

🍃 2.3 Leaf

Tomato has compound leaves. The leaf consists of a petiole and a rachis bearing leaflets. Tomato foliage is an important photosynthetic organ and contributes directly to carbohydrate production required for vegetative growth and fruit development.

Leaf Structure

  • Petiole: Connects the leaf to the stem.
  • Rachis: Central axis bearing the leaflets.
  • Leaflets: Individual units forming the compound leaf.
  • Midrib and veins: Provide vascular support and transport.
  • Trichomes: Hair-like structures present on the leaf surface.

Important Features

  • Leaves are compound.
  • Leaf arrangement on the stem is spiral.
  • Leaflets may themselves show compound characteristics.
  • Stomata regulate gaseous exchange and transpiration.
  • Trichomes are abundant on tomato foliage.

🎯 PG/JRF Point: Tomato leaf is compound and contains leaflets arranged along a rachis. The leaf also contains stomata, vascular tissues and trichomes.

🌼 2.4 Inflorescence

Tomato flowers are produced in inflorescences. Depending on genotype and environmental conditions, the inflorescence may be simple or more complex/branched. The number and arrangement of flowers influence flowering and subsequent fruit set.

Important Features

  • Inflorescences arise from the shoot system.
  • Flowers are usually yellow.
  • Simple and branched inflorescence forms can occur.
  • Flower number can vary with genotype and environmental conditions.
  • A single inflorescence can contain flower buds, open flowers and developing fruits at different stages.

Tomato flowers and developing fruits on an inflorescence.

🔍 Observe: In tomato, an inflorescence may simultaneously show buds, open flowers and developing fruits because flowers within the same cluster do not necessarily develop at exactly the same time.

🌼 2.5 Flower — Detailed Structure

The tomato flower is a bisexual flower containing both male and female reproductive organs. It has the typical floral whorls and is adapted primarily for self-pollination.

Main Floral Parts

  • Pedicel: Stalk supporting the individual flower.
  • Calyx: Consists of sepals and protects the developing bud.
  • Corolla: Consists of yellow petals.
  • Androecium: Male reproductive whorl consisting of stamens.
  • Gynoecium: Female reproductive structure consisting of fused carpels.

Stamens

  • Stamens are the male reproductive organs.
  • Each stamen consists of anther and filament.
  • Anthers produce pollen.
  • Tomato stamens form a characteristic cone/cylinder around the female reproductive structure.

Gynoecium

  • The carpels are fused.
  • The ovary contains ovules.
  • The style connects the ovary with the stigma.
  • The stigma receives pollen.
  • After fertilization, ovules develop into seeds and the ovary develops into the fruit.

🐝 2.6 Pollination & Fertilization — Basic Plant Description

Tomato is predominantly self-pollinated. The position and structure of the stamens and stigma favour self-pollination, although environmental conditions and flower morphology can influence the efficiency of pollen release and fruit set.

  • Pollen is produced in the anthers.
  • Pollen must reach the receptive stigma for successful fertilization.
  • After pollen germination, the pollen tube grows toward the ovule.
  • Successful fertilization initiates seed and fruit development.
  • Temperature, humidity and flower condition influence pollen viability and fruit set.
📌 Important: Pollination, fertilization and fruit set are connected processes but are not identical. Successful pollination is followed by fertilization and subsequent fruit development.

🍅 2.7 Fruit

Botanically, tomato is a fleshy berry. The fruit develops from the ovary after successful fertilization. Its internal structure consists of the pericarp, locules, placenta and seeds.

Fruit Structure

  • Pericarp: Fleshy fruit wall.
  • Locules: Internal chambers containing seeds.
  • Placenta: Tissue to which the ovules/seeds are attached.
  • Seeds: Develop from ovules after fertilization.
  • Skin: Outer protective layer of the fruit.

Tomato cultivars may differ in the number of locules. Fruit morphology is therefore an important varietal and breeding character.

Tomato fruit cross-section showing internal locules and seeds.

🎯 PG/JRF Point: Tomato fruit is botanically a berry. The fruit develops from the ovary, while the seeds develop from ovules.

🌱 2.8 Seed

Tomato seeds develop from ovules following fertilization. Mature seeds are enclosed within the locules of the fleshy fruit and are surrounded by a gelatinous material.

Importance of Seed

  • Seed is the principal reproductive unit used for commercial propagation.
  • Seed quality directly influences germination and seedling establishment.
  • Healthy and genetically pure seed is important for uniform crop production.
  • Seed treatment and proper nursery management are important before transplanting.
🧠 Remember the sequence: Flower → Pollination → Fertilization → Ovule becomes Seed → Ovary develops into Fruit.

3. Flowering, Pollination & Fertilization

Flowering is the beginning of the reproductive phase of tomato. Successful flowering, pollination and fertilization are essential for fruit set, seed development and ultimately marketable yield.

🌼 3.1 Flowering in Tomato

Tomato produces flowers in clusters called inflorescences. Flowering behaviour is influenced by genotype, plant age, temperature, light, nutrition, water availability and overall plant health.

Flower Initiation

  • Flower initiation represents the transition of a growing shoot toward reproductive development.
  • The first inflorescence generally develops after a certain number of vegetative leaves/nodes depending on genotype and growing conditions.
  • Subsequent flowering occurs through the characteristic sympodial growth pattern.
  • Determinate and indeterminate cultivars differ in duration and pattern of flowering.

Factors Affecting Flowering

  • Temperature: Very high or very low temperatures can interfere with normal flowering.
  • Light: Adequate light is important for healthy reproductive growth.
  • Nutrition: Balanced nutrition supports flower initiation and development.
  • Water: Both drought stress and excessive moisture can negatively affect reproductive growth.
  • Plant health: Pest, disease and severe physiological stress can reduce flowering.
  • Genotype: Varieties and hybrids differ in flowering behaviour.
🎯 PG/JRF Point: Flowering is not controlled by a single factor. Temperature, light, genotype, nutrition, water status and plant health interact to determine flower production and reproductive success.

🌼 3.2 Reproductive Structure of the Tomato Flower

The tomato flower is bisexual, meaning that both male and female reproductive organs are present in the same flower.

Tomato flower: identify anther, filament, stigma, style, ovary, petals and sepals.

Male Reproductive Part — Androecium

  • Anther: Produces pollen grains.
  • Filament: Supports the anther.
  • Tomato anthers are closely arranged around the female reproductive structure.
  • The arrangement of the stamens contributes to the predominantly self-pollinated nature of tomato.

Female Reproductive Part — Gynoecium

  • Stigma: Receives pollen grains.
  • Style: Connects stigma with the ovary.
  • Ovary: Contains ovules and later develops into the fruit.
  • Ovule: Develops into a seed after successful fertilization.
🧠 Remember: Anther → Pollen Stigma → Receives pollen Style → Pollen-tube pathway Ovary → Fruit Ovule → Seed

🔬 3.3 Pollen Production & Pollen Viability

Pollen grains are produced inside the anthers. For successful fertilization, pollen must remain viable, reach the stigma and germinate under suitable conditions.

Important Factors Affecting Pollen Performance

  • Temperature
  • Relative humidity
  • Flower age
  • Plant nutrition
  • Water stress
  • Genotype
  • Environmental stress
⚠️ Important: Poor pollen viability can lead to poor pollination and consequently poor fruit set even when flowers are produced normally.

🌼 3.4 Pollination in Tomato

Tomato is predominantly self-pollinated. Pollen from the anthers of a flower can reach the stigma of the same flower because of the close relationship between the male and female reproductive structures.

How Pollination Occurs

  • Pollen grains are released from the anthers.
  • Pollen comes into contact with the receptive stigma.
  • The pollen grain germinates on the stigma.
  • A pollen tube develops and grows through the style.
  • The pollen tube reaches the ovary and ultimately the ovule.

Role of Vibration

Tomato pollen release can be assisted by vibration. In protected cultivation, suitable mechanical or biological pollination techniques may therefore be used to improve pollen release and fruit set.

Pollination Assistance Methods

  • Natural movement of flowers and environmental vibration.
  • Manual shaking of flower clusters where appropriate.
  • Mechanical vibration tools in protected cultivation where suitable.
  • Appropriate pollinating insects, particularly bumblebees, may be used in some protected production systems.

Pollination pathway: Anther → Pollen → Stigma.

🎯 PG/JRF Point: Tomato is predominantly self-pollinated, but successful pollen release, pollen transfer and stigma receptivity are essential for good fruit set.

🔬 3.5 Stigma Receptivity

For successful pollination, the stigma must be receptive when viable pollen reaches it. Stigma receptivity is influenced by flower age, temperature, humidity and other environmental conditions.

  • Receptive stigma supports pollen germination.
  • Very poor environmental conditions can reduce successful pollen tube growth.
  • Flower age affects reproductive success.
  • Healthy flowers are more likely to produce successful fruit set.

🌱 3.6 Fertilization

After a compatible pollen grain lands on the stigma, it germinates and produces a pollen tube. The pollen tube grows through the style toward the ovary and reaches the ovule. Fertilization results in the beginning of seed development and initiates the sequence leading to fruit development.

Fertilization Sequence

1. Pollen Landing Pollen reaches the receptive stigma.
2. Germination Pollen grain germinates on the stigma.
3. Pollen Tube Pollen tube grows through the style.
4. Ovule Pollen tube reaches the ovule.
5. Fertilization Male gamete fuses with the female gamete.
6. Seed Development The fertilized ovule develops into a seed.

Pollen germination and pollen-tube growth toward the ovule.

🍅 3.7 Fruit Set

Fruit set is the successful transition of a flower into a developing fruit following successful reproductive events. It is one of the most important stages determining final tomato yield.

Factors Affecting Fruit Set

  • Successful pollination
  • Pollen viability
  • Stigma receptivity
  • Temperature
  • Relative humidity
  • Water availability
  • Balanced nutrition
  • Plant growth and vigour
  • Genotype
  • Pest and disease pressure

Flower → successful pollination → fertilization → fruit set → fruit development.

⚠️ 3.8 Poor Fruit Set & Flower Drop

Tomato may produce flowers without obtaining satisfactory fruit set. This can occur when environmental or physiological conditions interfere with pollen production, pollen release, pollen germination, fertilization or early fruit development.

Major Causes

  • Very high or very low temperatures
  • Temperature fluctuations
  • Low pollen viability
  • Poor pollen release
  • Inadequate pollination
  • Water stress
  • Excessive vegetative growth
  • Nutritional imbalance
  • Severe pest or disease attack
  • General plant stress

Management Principles

  • Maintain suitable temperature and humidity as far as possible.
  • Maintain uniform soil moisture.
  • Provide balanced crop nutrition.
  • Maintain healthy plant growth.
  • Improve pollination under protected cultivation when required.
  • Manage pests and diseases before they severely affect flowering.

🧪 3.9 Plant Growth Regulators & Fruit Set

Plant growth regulators can influence reproductive development and fruit development in some horticultural situations. Their use depends on crop stage, cultivar, production system and local recommendations.

Important Concepts

  • Auxins: Can influence fruit development and fruit-set processes in appropriate situations.
  • Gibberellins: Influence growth and developmental processes.
  • Growth regulators should never be applied blindly because concentration and timing can strongly affect the response.
  • Use only registered products and recommended concentrations for the specific crop and purpose.
⚠️ Exam Note: Plant growth regulators can influence fruit set and development, but their effect depends strongly on concentration, application stage, cultivar and environmental conditions.

4. Fruit Development, Maturity & Nutritional Composition

Tomato fruit development begins after successful fruit set and continues through several stages of cell division, cell enlargement, seed development, colour development and ripening. Fruit size, shape, colour, firmness and nutritional quality are influenced by genotype, environment, water, nutrition and crop management.

🍅 4.1 Fruit Formation

After successful pollination and fertilization, the ovary begins to develop into the tomato fruit. The ovules develop into seeds while the ovary wall develops into the fruit wall. Early fruit growth involves rapid cell division followed by cell enlargement.

🔬 4.2 Tomato Fruit Structure

Botanically, tomato is a fleshy berry. The internal structure of the fruit contains the outer skin, fleshy pericarp, locules, placenta and seeds.

Major Parts

  • Skin: Outer protective covering of the fruit.
  • Pericarp: Fleshy fruit wall surrounding the internal chambers.
  • Locules: Internal chambers containing seeds and surrounding tissues.
  • Placenta: Tissue associated with attachment of the seeds.
  • Seeds: Develop from ovules after fertilization.
  • Gelatinous tissue: Surrounds the seeds within the locules.

🎯 JRF Point: Tomato fruit is botanically a berry. The ovary develops into the fruit and the ovules develop into seeds.

📈 4.3 Fruit Growth & Development

Tomato fruit growth occurs through a sequence of physiological changes. Early development is associated with active cell division, followed by cell enlargement and accumulation of water, sugars, organic acids, pigments and other compounds.

Major Stages

  • Early fruit set: Small fruit begins to develop.
  • Cell division: Number of cells increases rapidly during early development.
  • Cell enlargement: Cells increase in size and contribute greatly to fruit expansion.
  • Seed development: Seeds develop inside the locules.
  • Fruit enlargement: Fruit accumulates water and dry matter.
  • Ripening: Major changes occur in colour, texture, flavour and aroma.

🍅 4.4 Fruit Shape & Morphology

Tomato cultivars and hybrids show considerable variation in fruit shape and size. Fruit shape is an important varietal, breeding and market character.

  • Round
  • Flattened
  • Oval
  • Oblong
  • Elongated
  • Pear-shaped
  • Other cultivar-specific shapes

🎨 4.5 Fruit Colour Development

Immature tomato fruits are generally green because chlorophyll is dominant. During ripening, chlorophyll decreases and carotenoid pigments become increasingly important, producing the characteristic colour of ripe fruits.

Important Pigments

  • Chlorophyll: Associated mainly with green immature fruit.
  • Lycopene: Major pigment associated with the red colour of many ripe tomato cultivars.
  • β-carotene: Important carotenoid present in tomato and contributes to pigmentation and nutritional value.
🎯 Important: Lycopene is one of the most important carotenoids associated with the red colour and nutritional importance of ripe tomato fruit.

5. Climate & Soil Requirements

Tomato is a warm-season vegetable crop, but successful production depends on maintaining suitable temperature, light, moisture, soil conditions and balanced nutrition. Climate and soil conditions strongly influence vegetative growth, flowering, fruit set, fruit quality and final yield.

🌡️ 5.1 Temperature Requirement

Temperature is one of the most important environmental factors affecting tomato growth, flowering, pollen performance, fruit set and ripening. Tomato performs best under moderate temperatures. Excessive heat or cold can interfere with reproductive development.

Temperature Effects on Different Stages

🌱 Germination Suitable warmth promotes rapid and uniform seed germination.
🌿 Vegetative Growth Moderate temperature supports healthy leaf and stem development.
🌼 Flowering Temperature stress during flowering can affect pollen production and flower retention.
🍅 Fruit Set Extreme temperature can reduce pollen viability and successful fruit set.
🍅 Fruit Development Temperature influences fruit enlargement and biochemical development.
🔴 Ripening Temperature affects pigment formation, colour development and fruit quality.

⚠️ High Temperature Stress

  • May reduce pollen viability.
  • May interfere with pollen germination and pollen-tube growth.
  • Can reduce fruit set.
  • May increase flower drop.
  • Can affect fruit colour development.
  • May increase physiological stress and water requirement.

❄️ Low Temperature Stress

  • Slows plant growth.
  • Can delay flowering and fruit development.
  • May reduce pollen performance.
  • Can prolong crop duration.
  • Severe cold can cause chilling injury and poor plant establishment.
🎯 JRF Point: Temperature stress during flowering is particularly important because reproductive processes such as pollen viability, pollen release and fruit set are sensitive to environmental conditions.

☀️ 5.2 Light Requirement

Adequate light is essential for photosynthesis and healthy tomato growth. The plant uses light energy to produce carbohydrates that support vegetative growth, flowering and fruit development.

Importance of Light

  • Supports photosynthesis.
  • Promotes healthy leaf development.
  • Provides assimilates required for fruit growth.
  • Influences plant architecture.
  • Affects fruit colour and quality.

Low Light Conditions

  • Reduced photosynthetic activity.
  • Weak vegetative growth.
  • Lower carbohydrate production.
  • Possible reduction in flowering and fruit development.
  • Fruit may take longer to develop proper colour.

Excessive Light + Heat

  • Can increase leaf and fruit temperature.
  • Can increase water loss.
  • Exposed fruits may develop sunscald under severe conditions.
  • Heat stress can indirectly reduce fruit set.

💧 5.3 Relative Humidity

Humidity influences transpiration, pollen behaviour, disease development and water relations in tomato.

High Humidity

  • Can reduce evaporation and transpiration.
  • Prolonged leaf wetness can favour several diseases.
  • Very humid conditions can interfere with pollen release and flower performance.
  • Good ventilation becomes particularly important under protected cultivation.

Low Humidity

  • Increases transpiration and water demand.
  • Can contribute to water stress if irrigation is inadequate.
  • Severe water stress can affect flowering and fruit development.

🌧️ 5.4 Rainfall & Excess Moisture

Tomato requires adequate moisture, but excessive rainfall and prolonged waterlogging can seriously affect root health and crop productivity.

Problems Caused by Excess Moisture

  • Reduced soil aeration.
  • Root-zone oxygen deficiency.
  • Weak root development.
  • Increased risk of several soil- and moisture-associated diseases.
  • Nutrient losses and nutrient imbalance.
  • Difficulty in maintaining healthy foliage and fruit quality.

Management

  • Use well-drained fields.
  • Prepare raised beds or suitable ridges where necessary.
  • Provide proper drainage channels.
  • Avoid unnecessary irrigation after heavy rainfall.
  • Use protected cultivation where excessive rainfall severely limits production.

🌱 5.6 Soil Requirement

Tomato can be grown on several soil types when adequate fertility, drainage and moisture management are available. Well-drained soils with good organic matter and suitable pH are preferred.

Ideal Soil Characteristics

  • Well-drained soil.
  • Good soil structure.
  • Good water-holding capacity without prolonged waterlogging.
  • Adequate organic matter.
  • Good aeration.
  • Good nutrient-holding capacity.
  • Suitable soil depth for root development.

Preferred Soil Type

Sandy loam to loam soils with good drainage are generally suitable. ICAR material for tomato production under protected conditions gives a well-drained sandy loam with pH 6.0–7.0 as suitable for optimum growth and yield. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

🧪 5.7 Soil pH

Soil pH influences nutrient availability, microbial activity and root development. For tomato, a slightly acidic to near-neutral soil reaction is generally suitable.

Suitable Range Approximately pH 6.0–7.0
Low pH May increase the risk of certain nutrient toxicities and reduce availability of some nutrients.
High pH Can reduce availability of micronutrients such as iron and zinc.
Best Practice Test soil before planting and correct pH based on soil-test results.

🧂 5.8 Soil Salinity

Excess salts in the root zone can interfere with water uptake and nutrient balance. Tomato can show reduced growth and yield under excessive salinity.

Possible Effects

  • Reduced water uptake.
  • Reduced plant growth.
  • Leaf injury under severe conditions.
  • Reduced fruit yield and quality.
  • Nutrient imbalance.

Management Principles

  • Use good-quality irrigation water.
  • Maintain proper drainage.
  • Avoid unnecessary accumulation of salts.
  • Use soil and irrigation-water testing.
  • Select suitable tolerant varieties where appropriate.
  • Use mulching and efficient irrigation practices where suitable.

💧 5.9 Water Requirement & Drainage

Uniform soil moisture is particularly important during flowering and fruiting. Both severe water stress and excessive irrigation can reduce crop performance.

Critical Considerations

  • Maintain relatively uniform soil moisture.
  • Avoid repeated severe drying followed by excessive irrigation.
  • Flowering and fruiting require careful moisture management.
  • Drip irrigation can improve water-use efficiency.
  • Mulching can help conserve soil moisture and suppress weeds.
  • Drain excess water quickly after heavy rainfall.
ICAR guidance specifically emphasizes uniform soil moisture during flowering and fruiting and recommends drip irrigation for efficient water and nutrient management in suitable tomato systems. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

🌿 5.10 Basic Role of Macronutrients

Nitrogen (N) Important for vegetative growth, leaf development and protein formation.
Phosphorus (P) Important for energy transfer, root development and reproductive processes.
Potassium (K) Important for water regulation, enzyme activity, fruit development and quality.

🧪 5.11 Micronutrients — Role, Plant Part, Deficiency & Use

🔹 Zinc (Zn)

🔹 Iron (Fe)

6. Land Preparation, Nursery & Transplanting

Successful tomato production begins with proper field selection, soil preparation, quality seed, healthy seedlings and careful transplanting. Poor nursery management can result in weak seedlings, uneven crop establishment and reduced yield.

🌱 6.1 Land Selection

Selection of a suitable field is the first important step in tomato production. The field should provide good drainage, adequate sunlight, suitable soil and easy access to irrigation.

Important Points

  • Select a field with good drainage.
  • Avoid fields with prolonged waterlogging.
  • Choose fertile soil with good organic matter.
  • Prefer fields receiving adequate sunlight.
  • Carry out soil testing before planting.
  • Avoid fields with a history of severe soil-borne diseases where possible.
  • Maintain proper field sanitation.
  • Follow suitable crop rotation practices.
🎯 JRF Point: Good drainage is especially important in tomato because prolonged waterlogging reduces root-zone aeration and can increase the risk of root-related problems.

🚜 6.2 Land Preparation

The objective of land preparation is to produce a well-prepared, well-drained root zone in which tomato seedlings can establish rapidly and roots can explore the soil effectively.

Steps of Land Preparation

1. Primary Tillage Deep ploughing or appropriate primary tillage is used to loosen the soil and incorporate crop residues where suitable.
2. Secondary Tillage Harrowing and other suitable operations break clods and improve soil tilth.
3. Levelling The field is levelled to improve uniform irrigation and drainage.
4. Organic Matter Well-decomposed organic manure may be incorporated according to soil test and local recommendations.
5. Bed Preparation Raised beds, ridges or suitable planting beds may be prepared according to soil, season and production system.

⚠️ Important Precautions

  • Do not work the soil when it is excessively wet.
  • Remove diseased crop residues where appropriate.
  • Avoid creating compacted layers.
  • Ensure proper drainage before transplanting.

🌱 6.3 Seed Selection

Healthy and high-quality seed is essential for obtaining uniform germination and vigorous seedlings.

Characteristics of Good Seed

  • High germination percentage.
  • Good genetic purity.
  • Good physical purity.
  • Free from visible insect damage.
  • Free from seed-borne pathogens as far as possible.
  • Suitable variety or hybrid for the intended season and region.
  • Seed should be obtained from a reliable source.

Open-Pollinated Variety vs Hybrid

Open-Pollinated Variety Produces relatively stable progeny when maintained properly and may be suitable where farmers maintain seed according to recommended practices.
Hybrid Usually developed by crossing selected parents and may provide improved yield, uniformity or specific quality/disease-resistance traits.

🧪 6.4 Seed Treatment

Seed treatment is carried out to protect seeds and emerging seedlings from certain seed-borne and soil-associated problems and to support healthy establishment.

Objectives

  • Reduce seed-borne pathogen load.
  • Protect germinating seeds from certain soil-borne pathogens.
  • Improve early seedling establishment.
  • Reduce early nursery disease pressure.

Types of Seed Treatment

🌿 Biological Beneficial microbial agents such as suitable Trichoderma-based formulations may be used according to crop-specific recommendations.
🧪 Chemical Registered seed-treatment fungicides may be used where recommended for the specific crop and target pathogen.
⚠️ Safety: Use only registered products and label-recommended doses. Seed-treatment chemicals should not be used blindly or mixed without checking compatibility and label directions.

🪴 6.5 Nursery Site Selection

A healthy nursery is the foundation of a healthy tomato crop. The nursery site should be selected carefully to reduce disease pressure and ensure uniform seedling growth.

  • Select a clean and well-drained location.
  • Ensure adequate sunlight.
  • Provide access to clean irrigation water.
  • Avoid areas with standing water.
  • Avoid repeated use of heavily contaminated nursery soil.
  • Maintain proper sanitation around nursery beds.
  • Provide protection from extreme weather where necessary.

🌱 6.6 Nursery Bed Preparation

Tomato seedlings may be raised on properly prepared nursery beds or under protected nursery systems. Raised beds are useful where drainage is a concern.

Good Nursery Bed Characteristics

  • Fine and friable growing medium.
  • Good drainage.
  • Good aeration.
  • Free from visible weeds and debris.
  • Good moisture-holding capacity without waterlogging.
  • Suitable organic matter.

Nursery Media

Depending on the production system, nursery seedlings may be raised in prepared soil-based media or soilless media such as cocopeat-based systems. The medium should provide adequate aeration, moisture and support for root development.

🌱 6.7 Seed Sowing in Nursery

Important Steps

  1. Prepare a clean and suitable nursery medium.
  2. Moisten the medium uniformly before sowing.
  3. Sow treated seed at an appropriate shallow depth.
  4. Maintain suitable spacing to avoid overcrowding.
  5. Cover seed lightly with fine growing medium.
  6. Provide gentle irrigation.
  7. Protect the nursery from heavy rain and excessive heat where necessary.

Why Overcrowding Should Be Avoided

  • Produces weak and elongated seedlings.
  • Reduces air movement.
  • May increase disease pressure.
  • Creates competition for light, water and nutrients.

💧 6.8 Nursery Water Management

  • Maintain uniform moisture during germination.
  • Avoid excessive irrigation.
  • Avoid prolonged drying of the nursery medium.
  • Use gentle watering to avoid disturbing seeds and seedlings.
  • Provide good drainage.
  • Reduce unnecessary watering as seedlings become stronger before hardening.
🎯 Important: Excess moisture combined with poor ventilation can favour damping-off and other nursery disease problems.

🛡️ 6.9 Nursery Pest & Disease Prevention

Cultural / Sanitation

  • Use clean seed and clean growing media.
  • Remove diseased seedlings immediately.
  • Maintain proper drainage.
  • Avoid excessive irrigation.
  • Keep nursery surroundings clean.
  • Control weeds around the nursery.

Biological Approach

  • Use suitable biological agents where recommended.
  • Beneficial microbial products may be incorporated into an integrated nursery management programme.

Chemical Approach

  • Use only registered nursery pesticides/fungicides for the target problem.
  • Follow label dose and safety instructions.
  • Do not repeatedly use the same chemical mode of action when resistance is a concern.

🌿 6.10 Seedling Hardening

Hardening is the gradual adjustment of nursery-grown seedlings to conditions closer to the field environment before transplanting.

Purpose of Hardening

  • Improves seedling tolerance to field conditions.
  • Reduces transplant shock.
  • Prepares seedlings for greater sunlight and air movement.
  • Improves establishment after transplanting.

General Hardening Practices

  • Gradually expose seedlings to field-like environmental conditions.
  • Avoid sudden severe water stress.
  • Gradually reduce excessive nursery protection.
  • Maintain healthy seedlings during the hardening period.

🌱 6.11 Identification of a Healthy Seedling

🌿 Stem Strong, healthy and capable of supporting the plant.
🍃 Leaves Healthy green leaves without severe pest or disease symptoms.
🌱 Roots Well-developed and healthy root system.
⚖️ Growth Compact and vigorous rather than excessively elongated.

Reject Seedlings Showing

  • Severe disease symptoms.
  • Major insect damage.
  • Weak or severely elongated growth.
  • Root damage.
  • Severe yellowing or nutrient-stress symptoms.

🌱 6.12 Transplanting

Transplanting should be carried out when seedlings are sufficiently developed and the field conditions are suitable. The exact transplanting age depends on nursery system, variety/hybrid, season and local recommendations.

Steps

  1. Irrigate the nursery appropriately before transplanting if required.
  2. Select healthy and uniform seedlings.
  3. Prepare planting beds or ridges in advance.
  4. Make planting holes at the recommended spacing.
  5. Transplant carefully without excessive root damage.
  6. Place the seedling at an appropriate depth.
  7. Firm the soil gently around the root zone.
  8. Irrigate immediately or according to the production system.

⚠️ 6.13 Transplant Shock

Transplant shock occurs when seedlings experience stress during or after transfer from nursery conditions to the field.

Symptoms

  • Temporary wilting.
  • Slow establishment.
  • Temporary growth reduction.
  • Leaf stress under severe conditions.

Management

  • Use healthy, properly hardened seedlings.
  • Transplant during suitable weather conditions.
  • Avoid unnecessary root injury.
  • Maintain appropriate soil moisture.
  • Protect seedlings from severe environmental stress immediately after transplanting.
  • Fill gaps with healthy seedlings where necessary.

🔄 6.14 Gap Filling

Missing plants should be replaced early so that plant population remains as uniform as possible.

  • Inspect the field after establishment.
  • Identify missing or dead plants.
  • Replace gaps with healthy seedlings.
  • Maintain uniform irrigation and early crop care.

📏 6.15 Plant Geometry & Spacing

Spacing should not be treated as one universal number because it varies with cultivar or hybrid vigour, season, training system, soil fertility, irrigation and open-field or protected cultivation.

Spacing Decision Factors

  • Variety or hybrid
  • Plant vigour
  • Growing season
  • Training and pruning system
  • Soil fertility
  • Irrigation method
  • Open-field vs protected cultivation
  • Target plant population
🎯 JRF Point: Closer spacing increases plant population but may increase competition and canopy density, while wider spacing improves individual plant space but may reduce plants per unit area. Optimum spacing is therefore production-system specific.

🌿 6.16 Early Management After Transplanting

💧 Irrigation Maintain appropriate soil moisture without prolonged waterlogging.
🌱 Gap Filling Replace missing plants early.
🧹 Weed Control Prevent early weed competition around young plants.
🍃 Crop Monitoring Inspect plants regularly for pests, diseases and nutrient problems.
🌿 Mulching Can help conserve moisture, suppress weeds and reduce soil splash where suitable.
🛡️ Sanitation Remove severely diseased or damaged plant material where appropriate.

🌿 7.2 Non-Mineral Essential Elements

1️⃣ Carbon (C)

  • Category: Non-mineral essential element.
  • Main source: Atmospheric CO₂.
  • Main role: Forms the basic framework of carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and many other organic compounds.
  • Important process: Photosynthesis.
  • Main plant parts: All living tissues.
  • Management: Adequate healthy foliage and proper photosynthesis are essential for carbon assimilation.

2️⃣ Hydrogen (H)

  • Source: Mainly water.
  • Role: Important in photosynthesis, respiration and formation of organic compounds.
  • Plant part: Present throughout plant tissues.
  • Management: Adequate water availability is essential.

3️⃣ Oxygen (O)

  • Sources: Water and atmospheric oxygen.
  • Role: Essential for respiration and many metabolic reactions.
  • Plant part: All living tissues.
  • Root importance: Adequate oxygen in the root zone is essential for healthy root respiration.
  • Management: Avoid prolonged waterlogging and soil compaction.

🌱 7.3 Macronutrients

4️⃣ Nitrogen (N)

  • Major role: Vegetative growth, chlorophyll, proteins and enzymes.
  • Important plant parts: Leaves and actively growing tissues.
  • Deficiency: General yellowing, reduced growth and premature senescence of older leaves.
  • Excess: Excessive vegetative growth and possible delay in reproductive balance.
  • Common sources: Urea, ammonium fertilizers and other approved nitrogen fertilizers.
  • Application: Basal + split/top dressing or fertigation according to recommendation.
Tomato example: ICAR recommendations vary with variety, hybrid, season and production system. One ICAR 2025 advisory lists 115:100:60 kg/ha N:P₂O₅:K₂O for varieties and 250:250:250 kg/ha for hybrids under its specified recommendation.

5️⃣ Phosphorus (P)

  • Major role: Energy transfer, ATP formation and root development.
  • Important stages: Early establishment, root growth, flowering and reproductive development.
  • Deficiency: Poor growth and sometimes dark green/ purplish foliage.
  • Sources: DAP, SSP and other approved phosphate fertilizers.
  • Application: Commonly supplied substantially through basal application according to soil test and recommendation.

6️⃣ Potassium (K)

  • Major role: Water regulation, enzyme activation, photosynthesis, fruit development and quality.
  • Important plant parts: Leaves and developing fruits.
  • Deficiency: Leaf-margin chlorosis/necrosis and poor plant performance.
  • Sources: MOP, SOP and water-soluble potassium fertilizers where appropriate.
  • Application: Soil, fertigation or crop-specific foliar programme.

7️⃣ Calcium (Ca)

  • Role: Cell wall formation, membrane stability and growth of meristematic tissues.
  • Important parts: Young tissues, growing points and fruits.
  • Deficiency: Damage to young tissues and physiological problems such as blossom-end rot can occur.
  • Important point: Blossom-end rot is strongly associated with inadequate Ca delivery to developing fruit, often involving irregular water supply.
  • Sources: Lime, gypsum, calcium nitrate and other suitable calcium fertilizers.
  • Management: Maintain adequate soil moisture and correct diagnosed calcium deficiency.

8️⃣ Magnesium (Mg)

  • Major role: Central atom of chlorophyll.
  • Other role: Enzyme activation and photosynthesis.
  • Important part: Leaves.
  • Deficiency: Interveinal chlorosis, commonly beginning on older leaves.
  • Sources: Magnesium sulphate and dolomitic materials where appropriate.
  • Management: Apply according to soil/plant diagnosis.

9️⃣ Sulphur (S)

  • Role: Component of sulphur-containing amino acids and proteins.
  • Important processes: Protein synthesis and enzyme activity.
  • Deficiency: General chlorosis, often more visible in younger growth compared with nitrogen deficiency.
  • Sources: Gypsum, SSP, sulphate fertilizers and elemental sulphur where suitable.
  • Management: Soil testing and balanced fertilization.

🔬 7.10 Micronutrients — Complete 8 Nutrients

Micronutrients are required in very small quantities, but deficiency of even one essential micronutrient can affect growth, flowering, fruit set and yield.

🔟 Iron (Fe)

  • Role: Chlorophyll formation and electron-transfer reactions.
  • Important part: Young leaves.
  • Deficiency: Interveinal chlorosis of young leaves.
  • Common causes: High soil pH, poor Fe availability and certain soil conditions.
  • Sources: Ferrous sulphate or suitable chelated iron formulations.
  • Management: Correct soil conditions and use a suitable Fe source according to diagnosis.

1️⃣1️⃣ Manganese (Mn)

  • Role: Photosynthesis, enzyme activation and metabolic processes.
  • Important part: Leaves.
  • Deficiency: Interveinal chlorosis with small necrotic areas in severe cases.
  • Common source: Manganese sulphate.
  • Application: Soil or foliar application only when deficiency is identified and according to the formulation recommendation.

1️⃣2️⃣ Zinc (Zn)

  • Role: Enzyme activity, growth regulation and normal development.
  • Important part: Young leaves and growing tissues.
  • Deficiency: Small leaves, shortened internodes and stunted growth.
  • Common source: Zinc sulphate.
  • Foliar example: ICAR has reported ZnSO₄ @ 0.25% in a specific tomato micronutrient programme; another ICAR advisory reports 0.5% under its specified system.
  • Important: Do not treat these experimental/advisory values as a universal dose for every tomato field.

1️⃣3️⃣ Copper (Cu)

  • Role: Enzyme activity, photosynthesis and lignification.
  • Important parts: Young tissues and reproductive growth.
  • Deficiency: Young leaves may become distorted or chlorotic with reduced growth.
  • Source: Copper sulphate and suitable registered copper formulations.
  • Application: Use only when deficiency is diagnosed and according to the formulation recommendation.

1️⃣4️⃣ Boron (B)

  • Role: Cell wall development, reproductive growth, pollen function and fruit set.
  • Important parts: Growing points, flowers and developing fruits.
  • Deficiency: Poor reproductive growth, abnormal growing points and poor fruit set.
  • Sources: Borax, boric acid and other suitable boron formulations.
  • Foliar example: An ICAR-published tomato study evaluated 0.05% boron metalosate under a specific experimental programme.
  • Important: Boron has a narrow range between deficiency and toxicity, so dose must be followed carefully.

1️⃣5️⃣ Molybdenum (Mo)

  • Role: Nitrate reduction and nitrogen metabolism.
  • Important processes: Nitrogen utilization and enzyme activity.
  • Deficiency: Chlorosis and poor nitrogen utilization may occur.
  • Source: Ammonium molybdate is a common Mo source.
  • Tomato example: An ICAR Annual Report describes a specific Meghalaya tomato programme using soil ammonium molybdate @ 0.5 kg/ha with foliar ammonium molybdate @ 0.10% at 15-day intervals.
  • Important: This is a location-specific programme, not a universal tomato dose.

1️⃣6️⃣ Chlorine (Cl)

  • Role: Osmotic regulation, ionic balance and photosynthetic processes.
  • Important part: Throughout plant tissues.
  • Deficiency: Rare under normal field conditions.
  • Source: Chloride-containing fertilizers such as potassium chloride can supply Cl.
  • Management: Usually deficiency is uncommon; avoid unnecessary application because excess chloride can be harmful under saline conditions.

1️⃣7️⃣ Nickel (Ni)

  • Role: Essential component of the enzyme urease and important in nitrogen metabolism.
  • Important process: Urea metabolism.
  • Deficiency: Rare; symptoms can include poor growth and abnormal accumulation of urea-related compounds.
  • Source: Usually required only in very small amounts.
  • Management: Correct only when deficiency is diagnosed; indiscriminate Ni application should be avoided.

💦 7.12 Foliar Spray — Detailed Guide

Foliar nutrition can provide rapid nutrient correction in selected situations. The exact concentration depends on the chemical/formulation, crop stage and recommendation.

💧 Zinc Sulphate

  • Purpose: Zinc deficiency correction.
  • ICAR example: ZnSO₄ @ 0.25% in a specific tomato micronutrient programme.
  • Another ICAR advisory: ZnSO₄ @ 0.5% under a different specified programme.
  • Important: Do not mix these recommendations or assume one concentration is universally applicable.

💧 Boron

  • Purpose: Reproductive development and diagnosed boron deficiency.
  • Example: 0.05% boron metalosate was evaluated in an ICAR-published tomato experiment.
  • Important: Boron should never be over-applied because the safe range is relatively narrow.

💧 Molybdenum

  • Purpose: Correction of Mo deficiency and support of nitrogen metabolism.
  • ICAR regional example: Ammonium molybdate @ 0.10% foliar spray in a specific Meghalaya tomato programme.

8. 💧 Irrigation & Water Management in Tomato

Water management is one of the most important practices in tomato cultivation. Proper irrigation is necessary for plant establishment, vegetative growth, flowering, fruit set, fruit development and fruit quality. Both water shortage and excess water can reduce yield and quality.

🎯 Main Principle: Maintain adequate and reasonably uniform moisture in the root zone, especially during flowering and fruiting.

💦 8.1 Importance of Water in Tomato

  • Required for photosynthesis and plant metabolism.
  • Helps transport nutrients from roots to different plant parts.
  • Maintains cell turgidity.
  • Supports leaf and stem growth.
  • Important for flowering and fruit set.
  • Supports fruit enlargement.
  • Helps maintain fruit size and quality.
  • Prevents severe moisture stress.

🌱 8.2 Root-Zone Water Management

The objective of irrigation is not simply to wet the entire field. Water should be supplied in a manner that maintains suitable moisture around the active root zone.

  • Maintain adequate moisture near active roots.
  • Avoid prolonged waterlogging.
  • Avoid severe drying between irrigations.
  • Adjust irrigation according to soil type and weather.
  • Consider plant growth stage and crop canopy.
  • Drip irrigation can deliver water directly near the root zone.
ICAR Point: Drip irrigation is recommended in suitable tomato systems for efficient water and nutrient management. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

🌸 8.3 Critical Stages of Irrigation

🌱 1. Establishment Adequate moisture is essential after transplanting for root establishment and recovery from transplanting shock.
🌿 2. Vegetative Growth Adequate moisture supports leaf, stem and root development.
🌼 3. Flowering Moisture stress can adversely affect flowering and reproductive development. Maintain uniform moisture.
🌸 4. Fruit Set Adequate and uniform moisture is important for successful fruit set and early fruit development.
🍅 5. Fruit Enlargement Water demand becomes important for fruit growth and maintaining fruit size.
🔴 6. Fruit Development & Ripening Avoid severe moisture fluctuation because irregular water supply can affect fruit quality.

Management

  • Avoid excessive irrigation and waterlogging.
  • Maintain adequate calcium availability.
  • Use mulch to reduce moisture fluctuation.
  • Follow balanced fertilizer management.

9. 🌱 Plant Growth Regulators (PGRs) & Their Use in Tomato

Plant Growth Regulators (PGRs) are natural or synthetic compounds that influence plant growth, development, flowering, fruit set, fruit development and ripening when used in appropriate concentrations.

🎯 Important: PGR response depends on crop variety, plant stage, environmental conditions and concentration. A concentration suitable for one purpose may produce a different or undesirable response at another concentration.

🌿 9.1 What are Plant Growth Regulators?

  • PGRs regulate physiological processes of plants.
  • They may promote, inhibit or modify specific growth processes.
  • They are used in very small quantities.
  • The response depends strongly on concentration and application stage.
  • In tomato, PGRs are mainly studied/used for fruit set, fruit development, growth regulation and ripening-related processes.

🧪 9.2 Major Groups of Plant Growth Regulators

Abscisic Acid ABA Stress responses, stomatal regulation and dormancy-related processes

🌱 9.3 Auxins

🔬 Important Auxins

  • IAA: Indole-3-acetic acid
  • NAA: Naphthalene acetic acid
  • IBA: Indole-3-butyric acid

🍅 Role in Tomato

  • Influence cell elongation.
  • Influence fruit development.
  • May be involved in fruit-set responses under suitable conditions.
  • Auxin balance is important in reproductive development.
Important: Do not use a random auxin concentration. Fruit-set and other responses are highly concentration- and stage-dependent.

🧪 9.4 NAA — Naphthalene Acetic Acid

  • Full name: Naphthalene Acetic Acid.
  • Group: Synthetic auxin.
  • Major physiological role: Growth and fruit-development related responses.
  • Tomato importance: NAA has been investigated for fruit set and development under specific experimental and production conditions.
  • Application: Use only crop- and formulation-specific recommendations.
⚠️ Concentration: NAA should not be given as one universal tomato dose because the required concentration changes with formulation, variety, growth stage and purpose. Always follow the registered product label or verified institutional recommendation.

🌿 9.5 Gibberellic Acid (GA₃)

  • Full name: Gibberellic acid.
  • Group: Gibberellin.
  • Main role: Cell elongation and growth regulation.
  • Can influence shoot growth and developmental processes.
  • Its effect depends strongly on concentration and plant stage.

⚠️ Excessive Use

  • May promote excessive vegetative elongation.
  • May disturb the desired balance between vegetative and reproductive growth.
  • Improper concentration can produce undesirable growth responses.

🌿 9.6 Cytokinins

Important Examples

  • Kinetin
  • BAP — 6-Benzylaminopurine

Major Functions

  • Promote cell division.
  • Influence shoot development.
  • Delay some aspects of leaf senescence.
  • Interact with auxins in regulating plant development.

Tomato Importance

  • Studied for growth and developmental responses.
  • Can influence reproductive and fruit-development processes in appropriate treatments.

🍅 9.7 Ethylene & Ethephon

Ethylene

  • Ethylene is a gaseous plant hormone.
  • It plays an important role in tomato fruit ripening.
  • It influences colour development, softening and other ripening processes.

Ethephon

  • Ethephon is an ethylene-releasing compound.
  • It can be used in specific crop situations to influence ripening.
  • Use must follow the registered product label and crop-specific recommendation.
🎯 JRF Point: Tomato is a climacteric fruit and ethylene has a major role in its ripening process.

🌵 9.8 Abscisic Acid (ABA)

  • ABA is strongly associated with plant stress responses.
  • It regulates stomatal behaviour.
  • It participates in drought-response signalling.
  • It interacts with other hormones during plant development.
Tomato relevance: ABA is particularly important for understanding plant response to water stress and fruit-development physiology.

🌿 9.9 Brassinosteroids

  • Promote or regulate several growth processes.
  • Influence cell expansion and developmental responses.
  • Can participate in plant stress tolerance mechanisms.
  • Tomato research has investigated brassinosteroids for growth, stress and fruit-development responses.

🛡️ 9.10 Jasmonates

  • Jasmonic acid is an important signalling molecule.
  • Associated with plant defence responses.
  • Participates in responses to herbivory and environmental stress.
  • Also interacts with reproductive and developmental pathways.

🛡️ 9.11 Salicylic Acid

  • Important signalling molecule in plant defence.
  • Associated with systemic acquired resistance.
  • Can influence responses to biotic and abiotic stresses.
  • Tomato research has examined salicylic acid for stress and defence responses.

🌸 9.12 PGRs & Fruit Set in Tomato

Fruit set depends on pollination, temperature, humidity, plant nutrition, water status and hormonal balance. PGRs may influence fruit-set responses under specific conditions, but they cannot replace proper pollination, nutrition and environmental management.

  • Maintain adequate plant nutrition.
  • Avoid severe moisture stress.
  • Maintain suitable temperature and humidity conditions.
  • Ensure proper pollination conditions.
  • Use PGRs only when there is a specific agronomic reason and a verified recommendation.

10. 🐛 Insect Pests of Tomato

🎯 Pest #1 — Tomato Pinworm / South American Tomato Moth

Tuta absoluta is one of the most important and destructive insect pests of tomato. The larval stage damages leaves, shoots, flowers and fruits. Early identification and integrated pest management are essential for preventing serious crop loss.

🔬 10.1 Scientific Classification

Character Details
Common name Tomato Pinworm / South American Tomato Moth
Scientific name Tuta absoluta (Meyrick)
Order Lepidoptera
Family Gelechiidae
Major damaging stage Larva
Nature of damage Mining, boring and tissue destruction

🌿 10.2 Host Range

Tuta absoluta is primarily associated with tomato and is particularly destructive under tomato production. It can also develop on other suitable plants belonging mainly to the Solanaceae.

  • 🍅 Tomato — major host
  • 🌿 Other suitable solanaceous hosts may support development.
  • 🌱 Weed hosts can contribute to pest survival where suitable.
📌 Important: Continuous availability of suitable host plants can help the pest survive and multiply between cropping periods.

🔍 10.3 Identification of the Pest

  • The adult is a small greyish-brown moth.
  • Larvae are small caterpillars and are the destructive stage.
  • Larvae commonly remain concealed inside leaf mines.
  • Larvae may also enter shoots and fruits.
  • Fresh leaf mines and fruit entry holes are important field clues.

🖼️ Tuta absoluta — labelled identification chart showing Egg → Larva → Pupa → Adult

🥚 10.4 Egg — Identification & Biology

  • Eggs are very small and difficult to see individually in the field.
  • They are generally laid singly on suitable plant surfaces.
  • Eggs may occur on leaves, stems and other tender plant parts.
  • Fresh eggs are pale/whitish and become darker as development proceeds.
  • Incubation period varies with environmental conditions.
  • Warm conditions generally accelerate development.
🎯 Identification Point:

Because eggs are very small, field monitoring should not depend only on egg detection. Leaf mines, larvae, fruit damage and adult monitoring are also important.

🐛 10.5 Larva — Identification, Instars & Damage

  • Larva is the principal damaging stage.
  • Young larvae are small and pale.
  • As the larva develops, body colour becomes more noticeable.
  • The larva feeds inside plant tissues and may remain concealed.
  • Larvae can pass through several larval instars.
  • Larval development is strongly affected by temperature.
  • Mature larvae leave feeding sites for pupation.

🔬 Larval Behaviour

  • Young larvae enter plant tissues soon after feeding begins.
  • They form mines inside leaves.
  • They may migrate from leaves to shoots or fruits.
  • Fruit-boring larvae remain protected inside the fruit.
  • Concealed feeding makes late chemical intervention less effective.

🟤 10.6 Pupa — Identification & Pupation

  • The mature larva leaves the feeding site for pupation.
  • Pupation may occur in soil, plant debris or suitable protected plant locations depending on environmental conditions.
  • The pupa is generally brownish.
  • The pupal stage is non-feeding.
  • Developmental duration depends strongly on temperature and other environmental factors.

🦋 10.7 Adult — Identification & Behaviour

  • Adult is a small moth.
  • Body and wings are generally greyish to brownish.
  • Adults are much smaller than many common tomato fruit-boring moths.
  • Adults are mainly active around the crop canopy.
  • Adults mate and females lay eggs on suitable host plant parts.
  • Adult monitoring can be done using species-specific pheromone traps.

🔄 10.8 Complete Life Cycle

Complete metamorphosis:

🥚 Egg → 🐛 Larva → 🟤 Pupa → 🦋 Adult → 🥚 Egg

🥚 Egg Stage

  • Female lays eggs on suitable host plant parts.
  • Egg development is temperature dependent.
  • Egg hatches into a young larva.

🐛 Larval Stage

  • Larva passes through several instars.
  • This is the principal feeding and damaging stage.
  • Larva mines leaves or attacks shoots and fruits.
  • Mature larva prepares for pupation.

🟤 Pupal Stage

  • Mature larva stops feeding and enters the pupal stage.
  • Pupa develops into adult moth.
  • Pupal duration changes with environmental conditions.

🦋 Adult Stage

  • Adult emerges from pupa.
  • Adults mate.
  • Females lay eggs and the cycle begins again.

⏱️ Development Duration

The duration of individual stages and the total generation time are temperature dependent. Under favourable warm conditions the pest can complete multiple generations during a tomato cropping period. Therefore, a fixed single duration should not be applied to every field or season.

🎯 JRF Point: Tuta absoluta undergoes complete metamorphosis: Egg → Larva → Pupa → Adult. The larva is the main damaging stage.

🧬 10.9 Biology & Behaviour

  • Adults reproduce rapidly under favourable conditions.
  • Females lay eggs on suitable host tissues.
  • Larvae are concealed feeders.
  • Leaf mining is a characteristic feeding behaviour.
  • Larvae can shift from leaves to shoots and fruits.
  • Fruit infestation creates direct quality and market losses.
  • Continuous tomato cultivation can favour pest build-up.
  • Crop residues can provide opportunities for survival.

📅 10.10A Crop Stage & First Attack Timing

  • First attack: Tuta absoluta ka attack crop establishment ke baad early vegetative stage se shuru ho sakta hai, especially jab suitable tomato foliage available ho.
  • Transplanted tomato: Transplanting ke baad pest field mein establish hote hi young leaves par infestation shuru kar sakta hai.
  • Direct-sown crop: Direct-sown tomato mein attack ka exact day fixed nahi hota; seedling/early vegetative stage se regular monitoring start karni chahiye.
  • Flowering to fruiting: Flowering aur fruit-development stage par monitoring aur bhi important ho jaati hai, kyunki larvae shoots aur fruits ko bhi damage kar sakte hain.
  • Important: Attack ko sirf fixed number of days se predict nahi karna chahiye. Temperature, pest population, nearby infested crops, crop stage aur host availability attack timing ko affect karte hain.

🔍 Monitoring Rule

Crop establishment ke baad early stage se regular scouting aur pheromone-trap monitoring start karna better hai, taaki pest ki initial population detect hote hi IPM measures liye ja saken.

🪰 11. Whitefly — Bemisia tabaci

Scientific Name: Bemisia tabaci
Order: Hemiptera
Family: Aleyrodidae
Major Importance: Direct sap-sucking pest and an important vector of Tomato Leaf Curl Virus (ToLCV).

11.1 Introduction

Whitefly, Bemisia tabaci, is one of the most important insect pests of tomato. Adults and immature stages suck sap from the undersurface of leaves. Severe infestation causes yellowing, weakening and premature senescence of leaves. The pest also produces honeydew, on which sooty mould may develop. Its greatest importance in tomato is its ability to transmit Tomato Leaf Curl Virus.

11.2 Identification

  • Adult: Small, soft-bodied insect with yellowish body and white wings covered with waxy powder.
  • Egg: Very small, usually laid on the underside of leaves.
  • Nymph: Flattened, scale-like and mostly sedentary during immature stages.
  • Pupa/Pupal case: Found attached to the lower surface of leaves.
  • Preferred location: Lower surface of young and tender leaves.

11.3 Damage Symptoms

  • Adults and nymphs suck plant sap from leaves.
  • Infested leaves show yellowing and loss of vigour.
  • Heavy infestation may cause leaf curling and premature leaf drying.
  • Honeydew secretion leads to development of black sooty mould.
  • Plants may become weak and growth may be reduced.
  • Severe infestation can affect flowering and fruit development.
  • Transmission of Tomato Leaf Curl Virus can cause severe crop loss.
⚠️ Most Important Damage:

The major indirect damage caused by Bemisia tabaci in tomato is transmission of Tomato Leaf Curl Virus (ToLCV).

11.4 Life Cycle

  1. Egg: Eggs are laid mainly on the lower surface of leaves.
  2. First-instar nymph: Newly hatched crawler moves for a short period and then settles.
  3. Later nymphal stages: Nymphs remain attached to the leaf and suck plant sap.
  4. Adult: Winged adults emerge and move to other plants for feeding and reproduction.

13.1 Introduction

Aphids are small, soft-bodied sap-sucking insects that infest young leaves, shoots and tender parts of tomato plants. They occur in colonies and can multiply rapidly under favourable conditions. Both nymphs and adults suck plant sap, reducing plant vigour and causing distortion of young growth.

🌿 5.22 Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

Integrated Pest Management combines different compatible methods to keep pest populations below economically damaging levels while reducing unnecessary pesticide use.

  • Cultural: Use healthy planting material, proper spacing, field sanitation, weed management and balanced fertilization.

  • Botanical: Neem-based products may be used where appropriate and permitted under current recommendations.
  • Chemical: Use effective registered insecticides only when necessary and according to the current label.

The main objective of IPM is not complete eradication of every insect, but maintaining pest populations below economically important levels.

🔬 Identification

  • Jassids are small, wedge-shaped, active sucking insects.

  • Both nymphs and adults suck plant sap.
  • The insect is generally green to yellowish-green in appearance.

🌱 Plant Part Attacked

  • Primary: Leaves
  • Also: Tender shoots and petioles may be affected.
  • Fruit: No major direct feeding damage is normally associated with jassid.

📅 When Does Jassid Appear?

Jassid infestation can occur during the vegetative to reproductive growth period, particularly when tender foliage is available and environmental conditions favour sucking pests.

Regular monitoring should therefore begin from the early vegetative stage and continue throughout crop growth.

🔄 Life Cycle

  • Egg
  • Nymph
  • Adult

Nymphs and adults are the important sap-sucking stages responsible for crop damage.

⚠️ Damage Mechanism

Jassid nymphs and adults insert their piercing-sucking mouthparts into plant tissues and suck sap from leaves.

  • Removal of plant sap
  • Reduction in photosynthetic efficiency
  • Leaf yellowing and marginal injury
  • Reduction in plant vigour
  • Severe infestation may reduce growth and yield

🍃 Symptoms on Leaves

  • Yellowing: Leaves may develop yellowish discoloration.
  • Marginal curling: Leaf margins may curl upward/downward depending on severity.
  • Leaf scorching: Severe feeding can produce brownish or scorched-looking margins.
  • Premature drying: Heavily damaged leaves may dry and fall prematurely.
  • Reduced plant vigour: Continuous sap removal weakens the plant.

🍅 Symptoms on Fruit

Major direct fruit symptoms are generally not characteristic of jassid.

The major economic damage occurs through sap feeding on foliage and tender plant parts, which can indirectly reduce plant growth and yield.

🌤️ Favourable Conditions

  • Warm weather
  • Presence of tender foliage
  • Dense crop canopy
  • Excessive nitrogenous growth
  • Presence of alternate weed hosts
  • Low monitoring and delayed management

  • Look for nymphs moving sideways.
  • Check young and tender leaves first.
  • Record increasing population before deciding on chemical intervention.
  • Yellow sticky traps may be used as a supplementary monitoring tool for flying sucking-pest populations.

🌾 Cultural Management

  • Maintain proper plant spacing and avoid excessive canopy density.
  • Avoid excessive application of nitrogen fertilizer.
  • Remove important weed hosts from the crop surroundings.
  • Maintain balanced irrigation and nutrition.
  • Use healthy and vigorous seedlings.

🛠️ Mechanical & Physical Management

  • Regularly inspect the lower surface of leaves.
  • Remove and destroy severely infested leaves when infestation is localized.

  • Adult females are wingless and remain associated with plant parts.
  • Colonies are commonly found on tender shoots, leaves, petioles, stem nodes and sometimes fruits.
  • Honeydew produced during feeding may result in ant activity and development of sooty mould.

🌱 Plant Parts Attacked

  • Leaves
  • Tender shoots
  • Stems and nodes
  • Petioles
  • Flowers
  • Young and developing fruits

Heavy infestation may spread from tender growth to several parts of the plant.

📅 Crop Stage / When It Is Seen

  • Mealybug can establish during the vegetative growth period when tender plant growth is available.
  • Population may increase during flowering and fruit development when infested shoots and plant parts provide suitable feeding sites.
  • Regular scouting should begin early and continue throughout the crop.
  • Weeds and alternate host plants around the field can act as sources of infestation.

🔄 Life Cycle

  • Egg / egg sac
  • Nymph / crawler
  • Adult female

The mobile crawler is particularly important for dispersal and colonization of new plant parts.

⚠️ Damage Mechanism

Nymphs and adult females suck sap from plant tissues using piercing-sucking mouthparts.

  • Continuous removal of plant sap
  • Reduction in plant vigour
  • Distortion of tender growth
  • Leaf yellowing and drying
  • Stunting of shoots
  • Honeydew deposition on plant surfaces
  • Development of sooty mould
  • Flower and fruit drop under severe infestation
  • Reduction in fruit development and yield

🍃 Symptoms on Leaves

  • White cottony colonies: Visible clusters of mealybugs occur on leaf surfaces and near veins.
  • Leaf yellowing: Continuous sap removal causes chlorosis and loss of normal green colour.
  • Leaf curling / wrinkling: Young leaves may become distorted, wrinkled or puckered.
  • Premature drying: Severely affected leaves may dry and fall.
  • Sooty mould: Honeydew deposited by the insect can support black fungal growth on leaf surfaces.
  • Reduced photosynthesis: Sooty mould and feeding injury reduce effective photosynthetic leaf area.

🌿 Symptoms on Shoots & Stem

  • White waxy colonies on tender shoots
  • Stunting of shoot growth
  • Distortion of young tissues
  • Weakening of tender branches
  • Heavy colonies around nodes and petiole junctions
  • Ant activity associated with honeydew-producing colonies

🌼 Symptoms on Flowers

  • Mealybug colonies may occur around flower clusters.
  • Feeding can weaken developing floral structures.
  • Severe infestation may contribute to flower drop.
  • Honeydew contamination may favour sooty mould development.

🍅 Symptoms on Fruit

  • Mealybugs may occur on young and developing fruits.
  • White waxy colonies may be visible around the fruit surface or near the calyx/pedicel region.
  • Honeydew deposited on fruit may encourage sooty mould.
  • Severe infestation can cause premature fruit drop.
  • Indirectly, reduced plant vigour can reduce fruit development and yield.

🌤️ Favourable Conditions

  • Warm weather
  • Dense plant canopy
  • Abundant tender plant growth
  • Excessive nitrogen-induced succulent growth
  • Presence of weeds and alternate hosts
  • Neglected or poorly monitored fields
  • Movement of infested seedlings or plant material

🔍 Monitoring & Scouting

  • Inspect young shoots, leaf axils, petiole junctions and undersides of leaves.
  • Look for white waxy colonies and mobile crawlers.
  • Check for ants because ants may be associated with honeydew-producing mealybug colonies.
  • Inspect weeds around the tomato field for possible source populations.
  • Mark localized infestation patches for early mechanical removal.
  • Use regular scouting rather than relying only on calendar-based spraying.

🌾 Cultural Management

  • Resistant/tolerant varieties: Where locally validated resistant or tolerant material is available, prefer it; do not claim resistance for a variety without reliable evidence.
  • Use healthy and pest-free seedlings for transplanting.
  • Inspect nursery plants carefully before transplantation.
  • Remove and destroy heavily infested plant material.
  • Maintain proper field sanitation.
  • Remove weeds and alternate host plants from inside and around the field.
  • Avoid excessive nitrogen fertilizer because excessive succulent growth can favour sap-sucking pests.
  • Maintain balanced irrigation and nutrition.
  • Avoid unnecessary movement of workers and farm tools from infested patches to healthy areas.
  • Do not carry infested seedlings or plant residues to clean fields.
  • Remove crop residues properly after harvest.
  • Use insect-proof nursery conditions wherever practical.

🛠️ Mechanical & Physical Management

  • Hand picking: Small localized colonies can be manually removed and destroyed.
  • Pruning secateurs: Heavily infested tender shoots can be pruned using clean secateurs.
  • Forceful water spray: A strong water jet can dislodge exposed colonies during early infestation.
  • Insect-proof nursery net: Helps reduce entry and spread of crawling/flying pest sources into protected nursery areas.
  • Yellow sticky traps: May be used as a supplementary monitoring tool for associated flying insects; they should NOT be considered a primary control method for adult female mealybugs.
  • Ant barriers: Where ants are actively tending mealybug colonies, physical exclusion or appropriate ant management can help disrupt colony protection.
  • Infested plant disposal bags: Removed infested material should be collected and safely destroyed rather than left in the field.

Important: Pheromone traps are not a standard primary management tool for mealybug; therefore they are not presented as a mealybug control method.

🦠 Biological Management

Predators:
  • Cryptolaemus montrouzieri (Mealybug destroyer)
  • Chrysoperla spp. (Green lacewing)
  • Ladybird beetles / coccinellid predators
Parasitoids:
  • Anagyrus spp.
  • Other locally established mealybug parasitoids may contribute to natural suppression.
Microbial / Biopesticide Options:
  • Beauveria bassiana
  • Lecanicillium spp.
  • Other registered entomopathogenic fungal products may be considered where the label permits use against the target pest.

Conserve natural enemies by avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum insecticide applications.

🌿 Botanical Management

  • Neem oil / azadirachtin-based products may be incorporated into an IPM programme where permitted by the registered label.
  • Horticultural/mineral oils may help suppress exposed mealybug stages where their use is permitted.
  • A suitable wetting agent may improve coverage where the product label allows it.
  • Because mealybugs have a waxy covering, thorough coverage of colonies is important.
  • Always follow the registered product label and check for phytotoxicity risk before large-scale application.

🏷️ Brand Name & Label Verification

  • Brand name alone does not determine mode of action.
  • The same active ingredient may be sold under multiple brands and formulations.
  • Always verify the active ingredient and formulation on the product label.
  • Only a brand with a current legal/registered claim for the relevant crop-pest combination should be presented as a field recommendation.
  • Do not use an old brand name or old dose simply because it appears in an older publication.

🔄 IRAC Resistance Management

  • Do not repeatedly use insecticides from the same IRAC group.
  • Avoid repeated use of Group 4A products such as imidacloprid and thiamethoxam in successive applications.
  • Rotate effective products belonging to different modes of action when chemical intervention is justified and legally registered.
  • Do not assume that changing the brand name changes the IRAC group.
  • Integrate biological, botanical, cultural and mechanical methods to reduce dependence on insecticides.
  • Avoid unnecessary calendar-based insecticide applications.
